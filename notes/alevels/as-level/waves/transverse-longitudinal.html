7.2

transverse and longitudinal waves

transverse waves

in a transverse wave, the particles of the medium oscillate perpendicular to the direction in which the wave travels.

a transverse wave can therefore be represented by a graph with displacement perpendicular to the direction of propagation.

examples include waves on a stretched string and electromagnetic waves.

longitudinal waves

in a longitudinal wave, the particles of the medium oscillate parallel to the direction in which the wave travels.

longitudinal waves consist of alternating regions of compressions and rarefactions.

a compression is a region where particles are closer together than normal, while a rarefaction is a region where particles are further apart than normal.

sound waves in air are an example of longitudinal waves.

graphical representation of transverse waves

a transverse wave can be represented using a displacement–distance graph. the vertical axis shows the displacement of particles from their equilibrium position, while the horizontal axis shows position along the direction of wave travel.

the maximum positive displacement corresponds to a crest, while the maximum negative displacement corresponds to a trough.

the wavelength \(\lambda\) is the horizontal distance between two adjacent points that are in phase, such as two successive crests.

the amplitude \(A\) is the maximum displacement from the equilibrium position.

$$\text{amplitude}=\text{maximum displacement}$$

graphical representation of longitudinal waves

longitudinal waves can also be represented using a displacement–distance graph.

the displacement shown is the displacement of particles parallel to the direction of propagation.

although the physical wave consists of compressions and rarefactions, the displacement–distance graph can be drawn as a sinusoidal curve.

regions where the displacement changes most rapidly correspond to compressions or rarefactions in the medium.

reading wave graphs

when interpreting a wave graph, first identify what each axis represents. a displacement–distance graph describes the shape of the wave at one instant, while a displacement–time graph describes the oscillation of one particle over time.

from a displacement–distance graph, the wavelength can be determined from the horizontal separation between equivalent points in successive cycles.

from a displacement–time graph, the period can be determined from the time between successive identical points in the cycle.

$$v=f\lambda$$

therefore, once the wavelength and frequency have been obtained from appropriate graphs, the wave speed can be calculated.

key distinction

the most important distinction is the direction of particle oscillation relative to the direction of wave propagation:

$$\boxed{ \text{transverse: oscillation}\perp\text{propagation} }$$

$$\boxed{ \text{longitudinal: oscillation}\parallel\text{propagation} }$$

summary

  • transverse waves have particle oscillations perpendicular to the direction of propagation.
  • longitudinal waves have particle oscillations parallel to the direction of propagation.
  • transverse waves have crests and troughs.
  • longitudinal waves have compressions and rarefactions.
  • electromagnetic waves are transverse.
  • sound waves in air are longitudinal.
  • transverse and longitudinal waves can both be represented graphically using displacement against distance or time.
  • wavelength is the distance between successive points in phase.
  • amplitude is the maximum displacement from equilibrium.
