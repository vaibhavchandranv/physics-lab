11.1

atoms, nuclei and radiation

the α-particle scattering experiment

rutherford's α-particle scattering experiment provided evidence that the positive charge and almost all of the mass of an atom are concentrated in a very small nucleus.

α-particles were directed at a thin sheet of gold foil. most passed straight through the foil, some were deflected through small angles, and a very small number were deflected through large angles.

the results showed that most of the atom is empty space, since most α-particles passed through without significant deflection.

the small number of large-angle deflections showed that the atom contains a tiny, dense, positively charged region capable of strongly repelling the positively charged α-particles. this region is the nucleus.

  • most α-particles pass straight through → most of the atom is empty space
  • some are slightly deflected → the nucleus exerts an electrostatic force
  • very few are strongly deflected → the nucleus is extremely small and dense

the nuclear radius is of order \(10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m}\), while atomic radii are of order \(10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}\). the nucleus is therefore tiny compared with the atom.

the nuclear model of the atom

the nuclear model describes an atom as a small, dense nucleus surrounded by electrons.

the nucleus contains positively charged protons and electrically neutral neutrons. electrons are negatively charged and occupy regions around the nucleus.

  • proton: charge \(+e\), mass approximately \(1\,\mathrm{u}\)
  • neutron: charge \(0\), mass approximately \(1\,\mathrm{u}\)
  • electron: charge \(-e\), mass approximately \(0.00055\,\mathrm{u}\)

in a neutral atom, the number of electrons is equal to the number of protons, so the total charge of the atom is zero.

nucleon number and proton number

protons and neutrons are collectively called nucleons.

the proton number, \(Z\), is the number of protons in the nucleus. it determines which element the atom is.

the nucleon number, \(A\), is the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus.

$$A=Z+N$$

  • \(A\) = nucleon number
  • \(Z\) = proton number
  • \(N\) = neutron number

$$N=A-Z$$

the number of neutrons can therefore be found by subtracting the proton number from the nucleon number.

isotopes

isotopes are atoms of the same element that have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.

because isotopes have the same proton number, they are the same element. however, they have different nucleon numbers because their numbers of neutrons are different.

for example, carbon-12 and carbon-14 both contain six protons, but carbon-12 contains six neutrons while carbon-14 contains eight.

nuclide notation

a nuclide can be represented using the notation

$$^{A}_{Z}X$$

  • \(X\) = chemical symbol of the element
  • \(A\) = nucleon number
  • \(Z\) = proton number

for example, uranium-238 is written as

$$^{238}_{92}\mathrm{U}$$

this means that the nucleus contains 92 protons and \(238-92=146\) neutrons.

conservation in nuclear processes

nuclear equations must obey conservation laws. two quantities that must be conserved are nucleon number and charge.

the total nucleon number \(A\) must be the same on both sides of a nuclear equation, and the total proton number \(Z\) must also be the same on both sides.

this provides a useful way of checking whether a proposed nuclear equation is physically possible.

$$\sum A_{\text{before}}=\sum A_{\text{after}}$$

$$\sum Z_{\text{before}}=\sum Z_{\text{after}}$$

radioactive radiation

radioactive nuclei can spontaneously emit radiation as they transform into more stable nuclei.

the three main types of nuclear radiation are α, β and γ radiation.

radiation composition charge relative mass
α helium nucleus +2 4
β⁻ electron −1 approximately 1/1836
β⁺ positron +1 approximately 1/1836
γ electromagnetic radiation 0 0

an α-particle is a helium-4 nucleus and therefore contains two protons and two neutrons.

β⁻ radiation consists of electrons emitted during a nuclear transformation, while β⁺ radiation consists of positrons.

γ radiation is high-frequency electromagnetic radiation. unlike α and β radiation, it has no rest mass and no electric charge.

antiparticles

every fundamental particle has a corresponding antiparticle.

an antiparticle has the same mass as its corresponding particle but opposite charge and opposite relevant quantum numbers.

the antiparticle of the electron is the positron.

  • electron: charge \(-e\)
  • positron: charge \(+e\)
  • both have the same mass

a particle and its antiparticle can annihilate each other, converting their mass-energy into other forms of energy, such as electromagnetic radiation.

β⁻ decay

in β⁻ decay, a neutron in the nucleus changes into a proton and emits an electron and an electron antineutrino.

$$n\rightarrow p+e^-+\bar{\nu}_e$$

the proton number increases by one, while the nucleon number remains unchanged.

$$^{A}_{Z}X\rightarrow{}^{A}_{Z+1}Y+{}^{0}_{-1}e+\bar{\nu}_e$$

the charge is conserved because the initial neutron has charge zero, while the final proton and electron have charges \(+1\) and \(-1\).

β⁺ decay

in β⁺ decay, a proton in the nucleus changes into a neutron and emits a positron and an electron neutrino.

$$p\rightarrow n+e^++\nu_e$$

the proton number decreases by one, while the nucleon number remains unchanged.

$$^{A}_{Z}X\rightarrow{}^{A}_{Z-1}Y+{}^{0}_{+1}e+\nu_e$$

the electron neutrino and electron antineutrino have extremely small masses and no electric charge. their role is essential for conserving quantities such as energy, momentum and lepton number in β-decay.

α-decay

in α-decay, an unstable nucleus emits an α-particle, which is a \(^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}\) nucleus.

$$^{A}_{Z}X\rightarrow{}^{A-4}_{Z-2}Y+{}^{4}_{2}\alpha$$

both the nucleon number and charge are conserved:

$$A=(A-4)+4$$

$$Z=(Z-2)+2$$

for example, uranium-238 undergoes α-decay to form thorium-234:

$$^{238}_{92}\mathrm{U}\rightarrow{}^{234}_{90}\mathrm{Th} +{}^{4}_{2}\alpha$$

energy of α and β particles

α-particles emitted by a particular nuclear transition have discrete energies. this is because the initial and final nuclei have specific quantised energy levels, so only certain energy differences are possible.

β-particles, however, have a continuous range of energies.

in β-decay, the available energy is shared between the emitted β-particle, the neutrino or antineutrino, and the recoiling nucleus. the β-particle therefore does not receive a fixed amount of the available energy.

the neutrino can carry away different amounts of energy from one decay to another, producing a continuous β-particle energy spectrum.

the unified atomic mass unit

atomic and nuclear masses are often measured using the unified atomic mass unit, written as \(\mathrm{u}\).

one unified atomic mass unit is defined as one twelfth of the mass of a neutral carbon-12 atom.

$$1\,\mathrm{u}\approx1.6605\times10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}$$

nuclear masses are conveniently expressed in atomic mass units because individual nuclei have masses of only a few atomic mass units.

summary

  • atoms contain a tiny, dense nucleus surrounded by electrons.
  • the nucleus contains protons and neutrons.
  • \(Z\) is the proton number and \(A\) is the nucleon number.
  • isotopes have the same \(Z\) but different numbers of neutrons.
  • α radiation consists of helium nuclei.
  • β⁻ radiation consists of electrons; β⁺ radiation consists of positrons.
  • γ radiation is electromagnetic radiation.
  • antiparticles have the same mass but opposite charge to their corresponding particles.
  • β⁻ decay produces an electron antineutrino.
  • β⁺ decay produces an electron neutrino.
  • nucleon number and charge are conserved in nuclear equations.
  • α-particles have discrete energies, while β-particles have a continuous energy range.
  • \(1\,\mathrm{u}\approx1.6605\times10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}\).
