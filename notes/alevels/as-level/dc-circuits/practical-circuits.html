10.1

practical circuits

circuit symbols

circuit diagrams use standard symbols to represent electrical components. using standard symbols makes circuits easier to draw, read, and interpret.

the symbols used in this syllabus include cells, batteries, switches, resistors, variable resistors, lamps, diodes, ammeters, voltmeters, thermistors, and light-dependent resistors.

circuit diagrams

a circuit diagram represents the electrical connections between components using standard circuit symbols and connecting wires.

components connected in the same continuous path are in series, while components connected across the same two points are in parallel.

electromotive force

the electromotive force, or e.m.f., of a source is the energy transferred by the source per unit charge in driving charge around a complete circuit.

$$\mathcal{E}=\frac{W}{Q}$$

  • $\mathcal{E}$ = e.m.f. (V)
  • $W$ = energy supplied by the source (J)
  • $Q$ = charge passing through the source (C)

e.m.f. is measured in volts, where one volt is equal to one joule of energy transferred per coulomb of charge.

$$1\,\mathrm{V}=1\,\mathrm{J\,C^{-1}}$$

e.m.f. and potential difference

e.m.f. describes the energy supplied per unit charge by a source to drive charge around the complete circuit.

potential difference describes the energy transferred per unit charge by a component as charge passes through it.

$$\mathcal{E}=\frac{\text{energy supplied}}{\text{charge}}$$

$$V=\frac{\text{energy transferred}}{\text{charge}}$$

therefore, e.m.f. refers to energy supplied by the source, while p.d. refers to energy transferred by a component.

internal resistance

a real source of e.m.f. has some resistance within the source itself. this is called its internal resistance.

when current flows, some of the energy supplied by the source is transferred within the source itself. this causes the terminal potential difference to be smaller than the e.m.f.

$$V=\mathcal{E}-Ir$$

  • $V$ = terminal potential difference (V)
  • $\mathcal{E}$ = e.m.f. of the source (V)
  • $I$ = current in the circuit (A)
  • $r$ = internal resistance of the source ($\Omega$)

the quantity $Ir$ is called the lost volts. it is the potential difference across the internal resistance of the source.

$$\text{lost volts}=Ir$$

if no current flows, $I=0$, so the terminal potential difference equals the e.m.f. of the source.

$$V=\mathcal{E}$$

for a given source, increasing the current increases the energy lost inside the source and therefore decreases the terminal potential difference.
