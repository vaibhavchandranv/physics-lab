8.1

stationary waves

principle of superposition

the principle of superposition states that when two or more waves overlap, the resultant displacement at any point is equal to the algebraic sum of the individual displacements.

$$\boxed{y=y_1+y_2+\cdots}$$

the waves continue to propagate through each other after the overlap, with each wave retaining its original properties.

stationary waves

a stationary wave is formed when two waves of the same frequency, wavelength and amplitude travel in opposite directions and superpose.

the resulting wave pattern does not travel along the medium. instead, certain points remain permanently stationary while other points oscillate with maximum amplitude.

nodes and antinodes

a node is a point on a stationary wave that always has zero displacement.

an antinode is a point that oscillates with the maximum possible amplitude.

nodes occur where the two waves always interfere destructively, while antinodes occur where they always interfere constructively.

$$\boxed{\text{node: amplitude}=0$$ $$\boxed{\text{antinode: amplitude}=\text{maximum}}$$

spacing of nodes and antinodes

adjacent nodes are separated by half a wavelength:

$$\boxed{d_{\text{node-node}}=\frac{\lambda}{2}}$$

adjacent antinodes are also separated by half a wavelength:

$$\boxed{d_{\text{antinode-antinode}}=\frac{\lambda}{2}}$$

a node and the nearest antinode are separated by one quarter of a wavelength:

$$\boxed{d_{\text{node-antinode}}=\frac{\lambda}{4}}$$

determining wavelength

the wavelength of a stationary wave can be determined by measuring the distance between nodes or antinodes.

if the distance between adjacent nodes or adjacent antinodes is \(d\), then

$$\boxed{\lambda=2d}$$

if a larger distance \(D\) contains several equal intervals between nodes, the wavelength can be determined by dividing the total distance by the number of intervals and multiplying by 2.

$$\boxed{\lambda=\frac{2D}{n}}$$

where \(n\) is the number of node-to-node or antinode-to-antinode intervals contained within \(D\).

microwave experiment

stationary electromagnetic waves can be demonstrated using microwaves. microwaves are reflected from a conducting surface, producing two waves travelling in opposite directions.

the incident and reflected waves superpose to form a stationary wave. regions of maximum and minimum signal can be detected using a microwave detector.

the distance between successive maxima or successive minima is \(\lambda/2\), allowing the wavelength of the microwaves to be determined.

stretched string

a stationary wave can be produced on a stretched string by driving one end of the string while the other end is fixed.

the wave travelling along the string is reflected at the fixed end. the incident and reflected waves then superpose to produce a stationary wave.

changing the frequency or tension of the string changes the conditions required for a stationary wave to form.

air columns

stationary waves can also be produced in air columns. sound waves travelling through the air column are reflected at the end, producing incident and reflected waves that superpose.

resonance occurs when the frequency of the source matches one of the natural frequencies of the air column, producing a large-amplitude stationary wave.

for this syllabus, end corrections are assumed to be negligible.

key relationships

  • adjacent nodes are separated by \(\lambda/2\).
  • adjacent antinodes are separated by \(\lambda/2\).
  • a node and adjacent antinode are separated by \(\lambda/4\).
  • the distance between adjacent nodes or antinodes can be used to determine \(\lambda\).
  • stationary waves are produced by the superposition of two identical waves travelling in opposite directions.

summary

  • the principle of superposition states that resultant displacement is the algebraic sum of individual displacements.
  • a stationary wave forms when two waves of the same frequency, wavelength and amplitude travel in opposite directions.
  • nodes have zero displacement at all times.
  • antinodes have maximum amplitude.
  • microwaves, stretched strings and air columns can be used to demonstrate stationary waves.
  • the wavelength can be determined from the spacing of nodes or antinodes.
