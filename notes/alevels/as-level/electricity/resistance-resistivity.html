resistance and resistivity

resistance

resistance is a measure of how strongly a component opposes the flow of electric current.

$$R=\frac{V}{I}$$

  • $R$ = resistance ($\Omega$)
  • $V$ = potential difference (V)
  • $I$ = current (A)

a component with a greater resistance requires a greater potential difference to produce the same current.

ohm's law

ohm's law states that the current through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across it, provided that physical conditions such as temperature remain constant.

$$V=IR$$

  • $V$ = potential difference (V)
  • $I$ = current (A)
  • $R$ = resistance ($\Omega$)

a conductor that obeys ohm's law has constant resistance when its temperature and other physical conditions remain constant.

i–v characteristics

an i–v characteristic shows how the current through a component changes as the potential difference across it changes.

for an ohmic conductor at constant temperature, the graph is a straight line through the origin because current is directly proportional to potential difference.

a semiconductor diode has a very small current when reverse biased and allows a rapidly increasing current when forward biased above its threshold voltage.

a filament lamp has a curved i–v characteristic. as the current increases, the filament becomes hotter and its resistance increases.

filament lamp

the resistance of a filament lamp increases as the current increases because the electrical energy transferred to the filament increases its temperature.

as the temperature rises, the metal ions in the filament vibrate more strongly. this causes more collisions with the electrons, increasing the resistance.

resistivity

resistivity is a property of a material that describes how strongly the material resists the flow of electric current.

the resistance of a uniform conductor depends on its resistivity, length, and cross-sectional area.

$$R=\frac{\rho L}{A}$$

  • $R$ = resistance ($\Omega$)
  • $\rho$ = resistivity ($\Omega\,\mathrm{m}$)
  • $L$ = length of the conductor (m)
  • $A$ = cross-sectional area (m2)

increasing the length of a conductor increases its resistance, while increasing its cross-sectional area decreases its resistance.

light-dependent resistor

a light-dependent resistor (LDR) is a component whose resistance changes with light intensity.

the resistance of an LDR decreases as the intensity of light increases.

thermistor

a thermistor is a component whose resistance changes with temperature.

the thermistors considered here have a negative temperature coefficient, meaning that their resistance decreases as their temperature increases.
